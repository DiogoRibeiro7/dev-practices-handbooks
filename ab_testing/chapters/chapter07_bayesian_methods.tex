\section{Introduction: Why Bayesian A/B Testing?}

The frequentist framework, dominant in classical statistics and covered in previous chapters, treats parameters as fixed but unknown constants. Statistical inference proceeds by asking: "If the null hypothesis were true and we repeated this experiment infinitely many times, how often would we see data as extreme as what we observed?"

This indirect reasoning—conditioning on hypothetical repetitions of an experiment we'll never actually repeat—can feel unnatural. When a product manager asks "What's the probability that variant B is better than A?", the frequentist framework cannot directly answer this question. P-values tell us something different: the probability of observing our data (or more extreme data) under the null hypothesis.

The Bayesian framework offers a more direct approach. It treats parameters as random variables with probability distributions that represent our uncertainty. We can make statements like "There is an 85\% probability that variant B's conversion rate exceeds variant A's" or "The expected revenue loss from choosing the wrong variant is \$12,000." These probability statements about parameters align naturally with how stakeholders think about uncertainty.

This chapter explores Bayesian approaches to A/B testing, from fundamental principles through advanced implementations. We'll see how Bayesian methods:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Provide intuitive probability statements about parameters
    \item Naturally incorporate prior knowledge from previous experiments
    \item Handle sequential testing without inflating error rates
    \item Enable risk-based decision making through expected loss
    \item Quantify uncertainty through full posterior distributions
\end{itemize}

We'll also examine when Bayesian methods are most valuable and when frequentist approaches remain preferable.

\section{Bayesian Fundamentals}

\subsection{Bayes' Theorem and Its Applications}

Bayes' theorem, first formulated by Reverend Thomas Bayes in the 18th century, provides a mathematical framework for updating beliefs in light of new evidence.

\begin{theorem}[Bayes' Theorem]
Let $\theta$ denote a parameter (or hypothesis) and $D$ denote observed data. Bayes' theorem states:
\begin{equation}
p(\theta | D) = \frac{p(D | \theta) \cdot p(\theta)}{p(D)}
\end{equation}

This can be rewritten as:
\begin{equation}
\text{Posterior} \propto \text{Likelihood} \times \text{Prior}
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $p(\theta)$ is the \textbf{prior distribution}: our beliefs about $\theta$ before seeing data
    \item $p(D | \theta)$ is the \textbf{likelihood}: probability of observing data $D$ given parameter $\theta$
    \item $p(D)$ is the \textbf{marginal likelihood} or evidence: $p(D) = \int p(D | \theta) p(\theta) d\theta$
    \item $p(\theta | D)$ is the \textbf{posterior distribution}: our updated beliefs about $\theta$ after seeing data
\end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

The marginal likelihood $p(D)$ serves as a normalizing constant ensuring the posterior integrates to 1. For parameter estimation, we often work with the unnormalized posterior:
\begin{equation}
p(\theta | D) \propto p(D | \theta) \cdot p(\theta)
\end{equation}

\begin{example}[Medical Diagnosis]
Consider a diagnostic test for a rare disease affecting 0.1\% of the population. The test has 99\% sensitivity (true positive rate) and 99\% specificity (true negative rate). If you test positive, what's the probability you have the disease?

Let $D = 1$ denote a positive test and $\theta = 1$ denote having the disease.

\textbf{Prior:} $P(\theta = 1) = 0.001$, $P(\theta = 0) = 0.999$

\textbf{Likelihood:} $P(D = 1 | \theta = 1) = 0.99$, $P(D = 1 | \theta = 0) = 0.01$

\textbf{Posterior:}
\begin{align}
P(\theta = 1 | D = 1) &= \frac{P(D = 1 | \theta = 1) \cdot P(\theta = 1)}{P(D = 1)} \\
&= \frac{0.99 \times 0.001}{0.99 \times 0.001 + 0.01 \times 0.999} \\
&= \frac{0.00099}{0.01098} \approx 0.090
\end{align}

Despite a positive test with 99\% accuracy, you have only a 9\% probability of having the disease! The low prior probability dominates. This illustrates the power of prior information in Bayesian reasoning.
\end{example}

\subsection{Prior Distributions and Their Selection}

The prior distribution $p(\theta)$ encodes our beliefs about parameters before seeing data. Prior selection is both an art and a science, requiring domain knowledge, mathematical convenience, and sensitivity analysis.

\subsubsection{Types of Priors}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Uninformative (Flat) Priors}

    These priors express minimal knowledge or "letting the data speak." For a parameter $p \in [0,1]$:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Uniform prior: $p \sim \text{Beta}(1, 1)$ (all values equally likely)
        \item Jeffreys prior: $p \sim \text{Beta}(0.5, 0.5)$ (invariant to reparameterization)
        \item Flat improper prior: $p(\theta) \propto 1$ (not a proper distribution but often yields proper posteriors)
    \end{itemize}

    \textbf{When to use:} True lack of prior knowledge, first experiment on a new metric, regulatory requirements for objectivity.

    \item \textbf{Weakly Informative Priors}

    These priors gently constrain parameters to plausible regions without strongly influencing results. For conversion rates, $\text{Beta}(2, 18)$ centers around 10\% but allows substantial uncertainty.

    \textbf{When to use:} Some domain knowledge exists but shouldn't dominate. Provides regularization to avoid extreme estimates with limited data.

    \item \textbf{Informative Priors}

    Strong priors based on historical data or subject matter expertise. For a metric with extensive history showing 10\% conversion (SE $\pm$ 1\%), use $\text{Beta}(50, 450)$.

    \textbf{When to use:} Extensive historical data exists, testing rare events requiring stabilization, sequential experiments where previous posteriors become new priors.

    \item \textbf{Empirical Bayes}

    Estimate prior parameters from data itself, typically from related experiments. If 100 previous A/B tests show average conversion of 8.5\% with variance 0.002, fit a Beta distribution matching these moments.

    \textbf{When to use:} Multiple similar experiments available, hierarchical modeling, platform-wide experimentation programs.

    \item \textbf{Skeptical Priors}

    Priors centered on the null hypothesis (no effect). Forces strong evidence to overcome skepticism. For lift, use $\text{Normal}(0, 0.02)$ centered at zero effect.

    \textbf{When to use:} High cost of false positives, extraordinary claims requiring extraordinary evidence, testing potentially harmful interventions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Mathematical Properties of Common Priors}

\textbf{Beta Distribution:}

The Beta distribution is the conjugate prior for binomial likelihoods (conversion rates):
\begin{equation}
\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta): \quad f(p; \alpha, \beta) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)} p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{\beta-1}
\end{equation}

Properties:
\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}[p] &= \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} \\
\text{Mode}[p] &= \frac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha + \beta - 2} \quad \text{for } \alpha, \beta > 1 \\
\text{Var}(p) &= \frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)} \\
\text{Precision} &= \alpha + \beta \quad \text{(inverse variance)}
\end{align}

Interpretation: $\alpha - 1$ can be viewed as "prior successes" and $\beta - 1$ as "prior failures." Total "prior sample size" is $\alpha + \beta - 2$.

\textbf{Normal Distribution:}

For continuous metrics (revenue, time spent):
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2): \quad f(x; \mu, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
\end{equation}

Conjugate prior for normal likelihood with known variance.

\textbf{Gamma Distribution:}

For rate parameters (purchases per user):
\begin{equation}
\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta): \quad f(\lambda; \alpha, \beta) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \lambda^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta\lambda}
\end{equation}

Conjugate prior for Poisson likelihood.

\subsection{Posterior Distributions}

The posterior distribution $p(\theta | D)$ represents updated beliefs after observing data. All subsequent inference flows from the posterior.

\subsubsection{Point Estimates from Posteriors}

Several summaries of the posterior distribution serve as point estimates:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Posterior Mean:}
    \begin{equation}
    \hat{\theta}_{\text{mean}} = \mathbb{E}[\theta | D] = \int \theta \cdot p(\theta | D) \, d\theta
    \end{equation}
    Minimizes expected squared error loss: $\mathbb{E}[(\theta - \hat{\theta})^2 | D]$

    \item \textbf{Posterior Median:}
    \begin{equation}
    \hat{\theta}_{\text{median}} = \text{Median}(\theta | D)
    \end{equation}
    Minimizes expected absolute error loss: $\mathbb{E}[|\theta - \hat{\theta}| | D]$

    \item \textbf{Posterior Mode (MAP):}
    \begin{equation}
    \hat{\theta}_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_\theta p(\theta | D)
    \end{equation}
    Maximum a posteriori estimate. With flat priors, equivalent to maximum likelihood estimate.
\end{enumerate}

For symmetric posteriors (e.g., normal), all three coincide. For skewed posteriors (e.g., gamma for rate parameters), they differ.

\subsubsection{Credible Intervals vs. Confidence Intervals}

This distinction is crucial for interpreting uncertainty quantification.

\begin{definition}[Credible Interval]
A $(1-\alpha)$ credible interval is an interval $[L, U]$ such that:
\begin{equation}
P(L \leq \theta \leq U | D) = 1 - \alpha
\end{equation}

The parameter $\theta$ lies in this interval with probability $1-\alpha$ given the observed data.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Confidence Interval]
A $(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval is constructed by a procedure that, over repeated sampling, contains the true parameter $\theta$ in $1-\alpha$ of realized intervals.
\end{definition}

\textbf{Key Difference:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Credible interval:} Direct probability statement about the parameter: "Given the data, there's a 95\% probability the conversion rate is between 4\% and 6\%."

    \item \textbf{Confidence interval:} Frequency statement about the procedure: "If we repeated this experiment infinitely, 95\% of constructed intervals would contain the true conversion rate."
\end{itemize}

The confidence interval interpretation is correct but indirect. Once we've observed specific data, the confidence interval either contains the true parameter or it doesn't (we just don't know which). The credible interval directly quantifies our uncertainty about the parameter given the data we observed.

\begin{example}[Credible vs. Confidence Interval Interpretation]
Suppose we observe 50 conversions from 1000 users and compute:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Frequentist 95\% CI: $[0.038, 0.067]$
    \item Bayesian 95\% credible interval: $[0.037, 0.066]$
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Incorrect confidence interval interpretation:} "There's a 95\% probability the conversion rate is between 3.8\% and 6.7\%."

\textbf{Correct confidence interval interpretation:} "The procedure used to construct this interval captures the true conversion rate in 95\% of repeated samples."

\textbf{Credible interval interpretation:} "Given our data and prior, there's a 95\% probability the conversion rate is between 3.7\% and 6.6\%."

For decision-making, the credible interval interpretation aligns with what stakeholders actually want to know.
\end{example}

\textbf{Highest Posterior Density (HPD) Intervals:}

For skewed posteriors, equal-tailed credible intervals (cutting 2.5\% from each tail) may not contain the most probable parameter values. HPD intervals contain the $(1-\alpha)$ highest density region:
\begin{equation}
\text{HPD}_{1-\alpha} = \{\theta : p(\theta | D) \geq k\}
\end{equation}
where $k$ is chosen such that $P(\theta \in \text{HPD}_{1-\alpha} | D) = 1-\alpha$.

HPD intervals are shortest among all credible intervals and contain the most probable parameter values.

\section{Bayesian A/B Testing Framework}

\subsection{Beta-Binomial Model for Conversion Rates}

The Beta-binomial model provides an elegant analytical solution for binary outcomes like conversions, clicks, or signups.

\subsubsection{Model Specification}

\textbf{Likelihood:}

For $n$ independent Bernoulli trials with probability $p$:
\begin{equation}
X | p \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)
\end{equation}

The likelihood function is:
\begin{equation}
p(X = x | p) = \binom{n}{x} p^x (1-p)^{n-x}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Prior:}

We place a Beta prior on the conversion rate:
\begin{equation}
p \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)
\end{equation}

\textbf{Posterior:}

The Beta distribution is conjugate to the binomial likelihood, yielding a Beta posterior:

\begin{theorem}[Beta-Binomial Conjugacy]
If $p \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ and $X | p \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)$, then:
\begin{equation}
p | X=x \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + x, \beta + n - x)
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior:
\begin{align}
p(p | x) &\propto p(x | p) \cdot p(p) \\
&\propto p^x (1-p)^{n-x} \cdot p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{\beta-1} \\
&= p^{x + \alpha - 1}(1-p)^{n - x + \beta - 1}
\end{align}

This is the kernel of $\text{Beta}(\alpha + x, \beta + n - x)$.
\end{proof}

\textbf{Intuitive Interpretation:}

The posterior parameters have a simple interpretation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Start with $\alpha - 1$ "prior successes" and $\beta - 1$ "prior failures"
    \item Observe $x$ successes and $n - x$ failures
    \item Posterior has $\alpha + x - 1$ total successes and $\beta + n - x - 1$ total failures
\end{itemize}

This is Bayesian updating in its simplest form: combining prior pseudo-observations with actual observations.

\begin{example}[Beta-Binomial Updating]
Consider three scenarios with uniform prior $\text{Beta}(1,1)$ and observed data of 80 conversions from 1000 users:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Uninformative prior:} $\text{Beta}(1, 1)$

    Posterior: $\text{Beta}(81, 921)$

    Mean: $81/1002 = 0.0808$

    95\% CI: $[0.0649, 0.0992]$

    \item \textbf{Weakly informative prior:} $\text{Beta}(5, 95)$ (prior belief around 5\%)

    Posterior: $\text{Beta}(85, 1015)$

    Mean: $85/1100 = 0.0773$

    95\% CI: $[0.0625, 0.0942]$

    \item \textbf{Strongly informative prior:} $\text{Beta}(50, 950)$ (strong prior belief of 5\%)

    Posterior: $\text{Beta}(130, 1870)$

    Mean: $130/2000 = 0.0650$

    95\% CI: $[0.0549, 0.0765]$
\end{enumerate}

Notice how stronger priors pull the posterior toward the prior mean. With 1000 observations, the data has substantial influence, but priors still matter. With 10,000 observations, even strong priors would be overwhelmed by data.
\end{example}

\lstset{language=Python, caption=Beta-Binomial Bayesian Inference}
\begin{lstlisting}
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import beta
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

class BayesianConversionRate:
    """
    Bayesian inference for conversion rates using Beta-Binomial model.
    """

    def __init__(self, prior_alpha=1, prior_beta=1):
        """
        Initialize with prior parameters.

        Parameters:
        -----------
        prior_alpha, prior_beta : float
            Beta prior parameters. Default (1,1) is uniform.
        """
        self.prior_alpha = prior_alpha
        self.prior_beta = prior_beta
        self.posterior_alpha = prior_alpha
        self.posterior_beta = prior_beta
        self.data_points = []

    def update(self, conversions, trials):
        """
        Update posterior with new data.

        Parameters:
        -----------
        conversions : int
            Number of conversions observed
        trials : int
            Number of trials (users/impressions)
        """
        self.posterior_alpha += conversions
        self.posterior_beta += trials - conversions
        self.data_points.append((conversions, trials))

    def posterior_mean(self):
        """Posterior mean conversion rate."""
        return self.posterior_alpha / (self.posterior_alpha +
                                       self.posterior_beta)

    def posterior_mode(self):
        """Posterior mode (MAP estimate)."""
        if self.posterior_alpha > 1 and self.posterior_beta > 1:
            return (self.posterior_alpha - 1) / (self.posterior_alpha +
                                                 self.posterior_beta - 2)
        return None

    def posterior_std(self):
        """Posterior standard deviation."""
        a, b = self.posterior_alpha, self.posterior_beta
        var = (a * b) / ((a + b)**2 * (a + b + 1))
        return np.sqrt(var)

    def credible_interval(self, alpha=0.05):
        """
        Equal-tailed credible interval.

        Parameters:
        -----------
        alpha : float
            Significance level (default 0.05 for 95% CI)

        Returns:
        --------
        tuple : (lower, upper) bounds
        """
        return beta.ppf([alpha/2, 1-alpha/2],
                       self.posterior_alpha,
                       self.posterior_beta)

    def hpd_interval(self, alpha=0.05):
        """
        Highest posterior density interval.

        Parameters:
        -----------
        alpha : float
            Significance level

        Returns:
        --------
        tuple : (lower, upper) bounds of HPD interval
        """
        from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar

        def interval_width(lower_tail):
            lower = beta.ppf(lower_tail, self.posterior_alpha,
                           self.posterior_beta)
            upper = beta.ppf(lower_tail + (1-alpha),
                           self.posterior_alpha,
                           self.posterior_beta)
            return upper - lower

        # Find lower tail probability that minimizes interval width
        result = minimize_scalar(interval_width, bounds=(0, alpha),
                               method='bounded')
        lower_tail = result.x

        lower = beta.ppf(lower_tail, self.posterior_alpha,
                        self.posterior_beta)
        upper = beta.ppf(lower_tail + (1-alpha),
                        self.posterior_alpha,
                        self.posterior_beta)

        return (lower, upper)

    def probability_greater_than(self, threshold):
        """
        Probability that conversion rate exceeds threshold.

        Parameters:
        -----------
        threshold : float
            Conversion rate threshold

        Returns:
        --------
        float : P(p > threshold | data)
        """
        return 1 - beta.cdf(threshold, self.posterior_alpha,
                           self.posterior_beta)

    def sample(self, n_samples=10000):
        """
        Draw samples from posterior distribution.

        Parameters:
        -----------
        n_samples : int
            Number of samples to draw

        Returns:
        --------
        np.array : Posterior samples
        """
        return np.random.beta(self.posterior_alpha, self.posterior_beta,
                            n_samples)

    def plot_posterior(self, ax=None, label=None):
        """
        Plot posterior distribution.

        Parameters:
        -----------
        ax : matplotlib axis
            Axis to plot on (creates new if None)
        label : str
            Label for the plot
        """
        if ax is None:
            fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 6))

        # Generate x values
        mean = self.posterior_mean()
        std = self.posterior_std()
        x = np.linspace(max(0, mean - 4*std), min(1, mean + 4*std), 1000)

        # Compute PDF
        y = beta.pdf(x, self.posterior_alpha, self.posterior_beta)

        # Plot
        ax.plot(x, y, linewidth=2, label=label or 'Posterior')

        # Add mean and credible interval
        ci = self.credible_interval()
        ax.axvline(mean, color='red', linestyle='--',
                  label=f'Mean = {mean:.4f}')
        ax.fill_between(x, 0, y, where=(x >= ci[0]) & (x <= ci[1]),
                       alpha=0.3, label='95% Credible Interval')

        ax.set_xlabel('Conversion Rate')
        ax.set_ylabel('Density')
        ax.set_title('Posterior Distribution')
        ax.legend()
        ax.grid(alpha=0.3)

        return ax

# Example usage
np.random.seed(42)

# Create Bayesian model with uniform prior
model = BayesianConversionRate(prior_alpha=1, prior_beta=1)

# Update with observed data
model.update(conversions=80, trials=1000)

print("Posterior Statistics:")
print(f"Mean: {model.posterior_mean():.4f}")
print(f"Mode: {model.posterior_mode():.4f}")
print(f"Std Dev: {model.posterior_std():.4f}")
print(f"95% Credible Interval: {model.credible_interval()}")
print(f"95% HPD Interval: {model.hpd_interval()}")
print(f"P(p > 0.10): {model.probability_greater_than(0.10):.4f}")

# Plot posterior
model.plot_posterior()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('beta_binomial_posterior.pdf')
plt.show()
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Normal-Normal Model for Continuous Metrics}

For continuous outcomes like revenue, time on site, or page load time, the normal-normal conjugate model provides analytical solutions.

\subsubsection{Known Variance Case}

\textbf{Model:}
\begin{align}
Y_i | \mu &\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2) \quad \text{(likelihood, known $\sigma^2$)} \\
\mu &\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \tau_0^2) \quad \text{(prior)}
\end{align}

\textbf{Posterior:}

\begin{theorem}[Normal-Normal Conjugacy with Known Variance]
Given $n$ observations $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \ldots, y_n)$ with sample mean $\bar{y}$:
\begin{equation}
\mu | \mathbf{y} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_n, \tau_n^2)
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{align}
\tau_n^2 &= \left(\frac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}\right)^{-1} \quad \text{(posterior variance)} \\
\mu_n &= \tau_n^2 \left(\frac{\mu_0}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n\bar{y}}{\sigma^2}\right) \quad \text{(posterior mean)}
\end{align}

Alternative form (precision-weighted average):
\begin{equation}
\mu_n = \frac{\frac{1}{\tau_0^2}}{\frac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}} \mu_0 + \frac{\frac{n}{\sigma^2}}{\frac{1}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}} \bar{y}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The posterior mean is a precision-weighted average of prior mean and data mean. As $n \to \infty$, the data overwhelms the prior and $\mu_n \to \bar{y}$.

\subsubsection{Unknown Variance Case}

When variance is unknown, we need a prior for both $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$. The normal-inverse-gamma is the conjugate prior:

\textbf{Model:}
\begin{align}
Y_i | \mu, \sigma^2 &\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2) \\
\sigma^2 &\sim \text{Inverse-Gamma}(\alpha_0, \beta_0) \\
\mu | \sigma^2 &\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \sigma^2/\kappa_0)
\end{align}

\textbf{Posterior:}

The posterior is normal-inverse-gamma with updated parameters. The marginal posterior for $\mu$ is Student's t:
\begin{equation}
\mu | \mathbf{y} \sim t_{2\alpha_n}\left(\mu_n, \frac{\beta_n}{\alpha_n \kappa_n}\right)
\end{equation}

This accounts for uncertainty in both mean and variance, yielding wider credible intervals than the known-variance case.

\lstset{language=Python, caption=Normal-Normal Model for Revenue Per User}
\begin{lstlisting}
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

class BayesianNormalModel:
    """
    Bayesian inference for normal mean with known variance.
    """

    def __init__(self, prior_mean=0, prior_var=1000, data_var=100):
        """
        Initialize with prior and data variance.

        Parameters:
        -----------
        prior_mean : float
            Prior mean
        prior_var : float
            Prior variance (tau_0^2)
        data_var : float
            Known data variance (sigma^2)
        """
        self.prior_mean = prior_mean
        self.prior_var = prior_var
        self.data_var = data_var
        self.posterior_mean = prior_mean
        self.posterior_var = prior_var
        self.n_obs = 0
        self.data_mean = 0

    def update(self, data):
        """
        Update posterior with new data.

        Parameters:
        -----------
        data : array-like
            Observed data points
        """
        data = np.asarray(data)
        n = len(data)
        y_bar = np.mean(data)

        # Update posterior parameters (precision-weighted)
        prior_precision = 1 / self.prior_var
        data_precision = n / self.data_var

        self.posterior_var = 1 / (prior_precision + data_precision)
        self.posterior_mean = self.posterior_var * (
            prior_precision * self.prior_mean +
            data_precision * y_bar
        )

        self.n_obs += n
        self.data_mean = y_bar

    def credible_interval(self, alpha=0.05):
        """95% credible interval for mean."""
        z = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha/2)
        sd = np.sqrt(self.posterior_var)
        return (self.posterior_mean - z * sd,
                self.posterior_mean + z * sd)

    def probability_greater_than(self, threshold):
        """P(mu > threshold | data)"""
        return 1 - stats.norm.cdf(threshold, self.posterior_mean,
                                  np.sqrt(self.posterior_var))

    def plot_posterior(self, ax=None):
        """Plot posterior distribution."""
        if ax is None:
            fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 6))

        sd = np.sqrt(self.posterior_var)
        x = np.linspace(self.posterior_mean - 4*sd,
                       self.posterior_mean + 4*sd, 1000)
        y = stats.norm.pdf(x, self.posterior_mean, sd)

\subsection{Comparing Two Variants: Bayesian A/B Testing}

The ultimate goal is comparing treatments. For variants A and B, we want to know:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $P(p_B > p_A | D)$: Probability B is better than A
    \item $\mathbb{E}[p_B - p_A | D]$: Expected difference
    \item Expected loss from choosing each variant
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Probability of Superiority}

For conversion rates with Beta posteriors:
\begin{align}
p_A | D_A &\sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_A, \beta_A) \\
p_B | D_B &\sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_B, \beta_B)
\end{align}

We want:
\begin{equation}
P(p_B > p_A | D) = \int_0^1 \int_0^{p_B} f(p_A | D_A) f(p_B | D_B) \, dp_A \, dp_B
\end{equation}

For independent Beta distributions, no closed form exists. Three approaches:

\textbf{1. Numerical Integration:}

For small $\alpha, \beta$ parameters, numerical integration is feasible:
\begin{equation}
P(p_B > p_A) = \sum_{i=0}^{\alpha_A - 1} \frac{B(\alpha_B + i, \beta_A + \beta_B)}{(\beta_A + i) \cdot B(1 + i, \beta_A) \cdot B(\alpha_B, \beta_B)}
\end{equation}

where $B$ is the beta function. This formula comes from expanding the integral.

\textbf{2. Monte Carlo Simulation:}

Simple and general:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Sample $p_A^{(1)}, \ldots, p_A^{(S)} \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_A, \beta_A)$
    \item Sample $p_B^{(1)}, \ldots, p_B^{(S)} \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_B, \beta_B)$
    \item Estimate $P(p_B > p_A) \approx \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^S \mathbb{I}(p_B^{(s)} > p_A^{(s)})$
\end{enumerate}

With $S = 100,000$ samples, Monte Carlo standard error is approximately $\sqrt{p(1-p)/S} \approx 0.0016$, giving high precision.

\textbf{3. Normal Approximation:}

For large sample sizes, Beta distributions are approximately normal:
\begin{align}
p_A | D &\approx \mathcal{N}(\mu_A, \sigma_A^2) \\
p_B | D &\approx \mathcal{N}(\mu_B, \sigma_B^2)
\end{align}

Then $p_B - p_A \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_B - \mu_A, \sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2)$ and:
\begin{equation}
P(p_B > p_A) = \Phi\left(\frac{\mu_B - \mu_A}{\sqrt{\sigma_A^2 + \sigma_B^2}}\right)
\end{equation}

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF.

# Comprehensive Bayesian A/B test analysis
bayesian_ab_test <- function(x_a, n_a, x_b, n_b,
                             prior_alpha = 1, prior_beta = 1,
                             n_simulations = 100000) {
  # Posterior parameters
  post_alpha_a <- prior_alpha + x_a
  post_beta_a <- prior_beta + n_a - x_a
  post_alpha_b <- prior_alpha + x_b
  post_beta_b <- prior_beta + n_b - x_b

  # Monte Carlo simulation
  set.seed(42)
  p_a_samples <- rbeta(n_simulations, post_alpha_a, post_beta_a)
  p_b_samples <- rbeta(n_simulations, post_alpha_b, post_beta_b)

  # Probability B > A
  prob_b_better <- mean(p_b_samples > p_a_samples)

  # Absolute difference distribution
  diff <- p_b_samples - p_a_samples
  diff_mean <- mean(diff)
  diff_median <- median(diff)
  diff_ci <- quantile(diff, c(0.025, 0.975))

  # Relative lift distribution
  lift <- (p_b_samples / p_a_samples - 1) * 100
  lift_mean <- mean(lift)
  lift_median <- median(lift)
  lift_ci <- quantile(lift, c(0.025, 0.975))

  # Probability of practical significance (e.g., >1% absolute lift)
  prob_practical_sig <- mean(diff > 0.01)

  # Expected loss (opportunity cost)
  loss_a <- mean(pmax(0, p_b_samples - p_a_samples))
  loss_b <- mean(pmax(0, p_a_samples - p_b_samples))

  # Posterior statistics
  stats_a <- list(
    mean = post_alpha_a / (post_alpha_a + post_beta_a),
    median = qbeta(0.5, post_alpha_a, post_beta_a),
    ci = qbeta(c(0.025, 0.975), post_alpha_a, post_beta_a)
  )

  stats_b <- list(
    mean = post_alpha_b / (post_alpha_b + post_beta_b),
    median = qbeta(0.5, post_alpha_b, post_beta_b),
    ci = qbeta(c(0.025, 0.975), post_alpha_b, post_beta_b)
  )

  # Return comprehensive results
  list(
    prob_b_better = prob_b_better,
    prob_practical_significance = prob_practical_sig,
    difference = list(mean = diff_mean, median = diff_median, ci = diff_ci),
    lift = list(mean = lift_mean, median = lift_median, ci = lift_ci),
    expected_loss = list(loss_a = loss_a, loss_b = loss_b),
    posterior_a = stats_a,
    posterior_b = stats_b,
    samples = list(p_a = p_a_samples, p_b = p_b_samples)
  )
}

\subsection{Expected Loss and Risk-Based Decisions}

Binary decisions based solely on $P(B > A)$ ignore effect magnitude. Expected loss provides a more nuanced decision framework.

\begin{definition}[Expected Loss]
The expected loss from choosing variant $i$ is the expected opportunity cost:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(i) = \mathbb{E}[\max(0, \max_j p_j - p_i) | D]
\end{equation}

For two variants:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}(A) &= \mathbb{E}[\max(0, p_B - p_A) | D] \\
\mathcal{L}(B) &= \mathbb{E}[\max(0, p_A - p_B) | D]
\end{align}
\end{definition}

\textbf{Interpretation:} $\mathcal{L}(A)$ is the expected loss (in terms of conversion rate or revenue) if we choose A but B is actually better. It quantifies regret.

\textbf{Decision Rule:} Choose the variant with minimum expected loss. This is optimal under expected loss criterion.

\textbf{Threshold-Based Decisions:}

In practice, we might only switch from A to B if expected loss exceeds a threshold:
\begin{equation}
\text{Switch to B if } \mathcal{L}(A) - \mathcal{L}(B) > \delta
\end{equation}

This builds in conservatism, accounting for implementation costs or risk aversion.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import beta
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def expected_loss_ab(x_a, n_a, x_b, n_b,
                     prior_alpha=1, prior_beta=1,
                     n_simulations=100000,
                     cost_per_user=1.0):
    """
    Compute expected loss and value-based decision metrics.

    Parameters:
    -----------
    x_a, n_a : int
        Conversions and trials for variant A
    x_b, n_b : int
        Conversions and trials for variant B
    prior_alpha, prior_beta : float
        Beta prior parameters
    n_simulations : int
        Number of Monte Carlo samples
    cost_per_user : float
        Value per percentage point of conversion rate

    Returns:
    --------
    dict : Comprehensive decision metrics
    """
    # Posterior parameters
    post_alpha_a = prior_alpha + x_a
    post_beta_a = prior_beta + n_a - x_a
    post_alpha_b = prior_alpha + x_b
    post_beta_b = prior_beta + n_b - x_b

    # Sample from posteriors
    np.random.seed(42)
    p_a = np.random.beta(post_alpha_a, post_beta_a, n_simulations)
    p_b = np.random.beta(post_alpha_b, post_beta_b, n_simulations)

    # Expected loss (in conversion rate units)
    loss_a_rate = np.mean(np.maximum(0, p_b - p_a))
    loss_b_rate = np.mean(np.maximum(0, p_a - p_b))

    # Expected loss (in monetary units)
    # Assuming cost_per_user is value of 1 percentage point gain
    loss_a_value = loss_a_rate * 100 * cost_per_user
    loss_b_value = loss_b_rate * 100 * cost_per_user

    # Relative expected loss
    rel_loss_a = loss_a_rate / np.mean(p_a) if np.mean(p_a) > 0 else 0
    rel_loss_b = loss_b_rate / np.mean(p_b) if np.mean(p_b) > 0 else 0

    # Probability of superiority
    prob_b_better = np.mean(p_b > p_a)
    prob_a_better = 1 - prob_b_better

    # Value at risk (VaR): 95th percentile of loss distribution
    var_95_a = np.percentile(np.maximum(0, p_b - p_a), 95)
    var_95_b = np.percentile(np.maximum(0, p_a - p_b), 95)

    # Expected value of perfect information (EVPI)
    # What would we pay for certainty?
    evpi = min(loss_a_rate, loss_b_rate) * 100 * cost_per_user

    # Recommendation based on minimum expected loss
    if loss_a_rate < loss_b_rate:
        recommendation = 'A'
        confidence = prob_a_better
        expected_regret = loss_a_value
    else:
        recommendation = 'B'
        confidence = prob_b_better
        expected_regret = loss_b_value

    return {
        'recommendation': recommendation,
        'confidence': confidence,
        'expected_regret': expected_regret,
        'loss_a': {'rate': loss_a_rate, 'value': loss_a_value,
                  'relative': rel_loss_a, 'var_95': var_95_a},
        'loss_b': {'rate': loss_b_rate, 'value': loss_b_value,
                  'relative': rel_loss_b, 'var_95': var_95_b},
        'prob_b_better': prob_b_better,
        'evpi': evpi,
        'loss_ratio': min(loss_a_rate, loss_b_rate) /
                     max(loss_a_rate, loss_b_rate)
    }

\subsection{Non-Conjugate Priors and MCMC}

When conjugate priors are unavailable or inappropriate, we need computational methods. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) provides a general framework for sampling from posteriors.

\subsubsection{Why MCMC?}

Bayes' theorem gives us:
\begin{equation}
p(\theta | D) = \frac{p(D | \theta) p(\theta)}{\int p(D | \theta) p(\theta) d\theta}
\end{equation}

The denominator—the marginal likelihood—requires integrating over all possible parameter values. In high dimensions or with complex likelihoods, this integral is intractable.

MCMC bypasses this problem by sampling from the posterior without computing the normalizing constant. We work with:
\begin{equation}
p(\theta | D) \propto p(D | \theta) p(\theta)
\end{equation}

MCMC constructs a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the posterior. After a "burn-in" period, samples approximate draws from $p(\theta | D)$.

\subsubsection{Metropolis-Hastings Algorithm}

The Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm is the foundation of MCMC:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Metropolis-Hastings Algorithm}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE Initialize $\theta^{(0)}$
\FOR{$t = 1$ to $T$}
    \STATE Propose $\theta^* \sim q(\theta^* | \theta^{(t-1)})$ \COMMENT{Proposal distribution}
    \STATE Compute acceptance ratio:
    \begin{equation*}
    \alpha = \min\left(1, \frac{p(\theta^* | D) q(\theta^{(t-1)} | \theta^*)}{p(\theta^{(t-1)} | D) q(\theta^* | \theta^{(t-1)})}\right)
    \end{equation*}
    \STATE Draw $u \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$
    \IF{$u < \alpha$}
        \STATE $\theta^{(t)} = \theta^*$ \COMMENT{Accept}
    \ELSE
        \STATE $\theta^{(t)} = \theta^{(t-1)}$ \COMMENT{Reject}
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\textbf{Key Points:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Proposal distribution $q$ suggests new parameter values
    \item Acceptance ratio ensures detailed balance (stationary distribution is posterior)
    \item Symmetric proposals (e.g., random walk) simplify acceptance ratio
    \item Tune proposal variance: too small yields slow mixing, too large yields high rejection
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Gibbs Sampling}

For multivariate posteriors, Gibbs sampling updates one parameter at a time:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Gibbs Sampling}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE Initialize $\theta_1^{(0)}, \ldots, \theta_k^{(0)}$
\FOR{$t = 1$ to $T$}
    \STATE Sample $\theta_1^{(t)} \sim p(\theta_1 | \theta_2^{(t-1)}, \ldots, \theta_k^{(t-1)}, D)$
    \STATE Sample $\theta_2^{(t)} \sim p(\theta_2 | \theta_1^{(t)}, \theta_3^{(t-1)}, \ldots, \theta_k^{(t-1)}, D)$
    \STATE $\vdots$
    \STATE Sample $\theta_k^{(t)} \sim p(\theta_k | \theta_1^{(t)}, \ldots, \theta_{k-1}^{(t)}, D)$
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Gibbs sampling is a special case of MH where proposals are always accepted (when using full conditionals).

\subsubsection{Hamiltonian Monte Carlo}

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) uses gradient information to propose distant moves efficiently. It's the engine behind Stan and PyMC3's NUTS sampler.

HMC introduces auxiliary momentum variables and simulates Hamiltonian dynamics. This reduces random walk behavior, enabling efficient exploration of high-dimensional posteriors.

\textbf{Advantages:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Efficient for high-dimensional problems
    \item Fewer correlated samples
    \item Faster convergence
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Disadvantages:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Requires gradient computation (automatic differentiation)
    \item More complex to implement
    \item Sensitive to step size tuning
\end{itemize}

\section{Practical Advantages of Bayesian A/B Testing}

\subsection{Intuitive Probability Statements}

The Bayesian framework directly answers stakeholder questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item "What's the probability variant B beats variant A?" \\
    $\to P(p_B > p_A | D) = 0.92$

    \item "What's the probability the lift exceeds 10\%?" \\
    $\to P(\text{lift} > 0.10 | D) = 0.15$

    \item "What's the expected revenue impact?" \\
    $\to \mathbb{E}[\text{revenue difference} | D] = \$45,000$

    \item "What's the risk if we choose the wrong variant?" \\
    $\to$ Expected loss = \$12,000
\end{itemize}

Compare to frequentist p-value: "If the null hypothesis were true and we repeated this experiment infinitely, we'd see data this extreme 3\% of the time." This doesn't directly address the questions decision-makers care about.

\subsection{Incorporation of Prior Knowledge}

Bayesian methods naturally leverage historical data and domain expertise.

\begin{example}[Leveraging Historical Data]
A company has run 50 previous A/B tests on similar features, showing an average 5\% conversion rate with standard deviation 2\%. For a new test, they can use this information to construct an informative prior:

Match a $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ prior to historical mean and variance:
\begin{align}
\frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} &= 0.05 \\
\frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)} &= 0.02^2
\end{align}

Solving yields $\text{Beta}(11.9, 226.1)$ (approximately).

With this prior and new data (50 conversions from 1200 users), the posterior is:
\begin{equation}
\text{Beta}(61.9, 1376.1)
\end{equation}

Posterior mean: $61.9 / 1438 = 0.043$

The prior stabilizes the estimate, preventing overfitting to small samples while respecting the new data.
\end{example}

\textbf{Benefits:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rare event estimation: Priors stabilize estimates when events are rare
    \item Sequential experiments: Previous posterior becomes new prior
    \item Segment-level tests: Share information across segments via hierarchical priors
    \item Regulatory compliance: Document expert knowledge formally
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Early Stopping Decisions}

Frequentist hypothesis tests assume fixed sample size determined a priori. Checking results early ("peeking") and stopping when significant inflates Type I error rates.

Bayesian inference doesn't suffer from this problem. You can check results anytime and stop based on posterior probabilities without invalidating inference.

\begin{example}[Early Stopping Rule]
Define a stopping rule:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Stop if $P(p_B > p_A | D) > 0.95$ (high confidence B is better)
    \item Stop if $P(p_B > p_A | D) < 0.05$ (high confidence A is better)
    \item Stop if $|\mathbb{E}[p_B - p_A | D]| < 0.002$ and $n > 5000$ (effect too small to matter)
    \item Stop if $n > 20,000$ (maximum sample size)
\end{itemize}

This rule balances statistical evidence, practical significance, and resource constraints. Checking daily doesn't inflate error rates in the Bayesian framework.
\end{example}

\textbf{Practical Implementation:}

\lstset{language=Python, caption=Bayesian Early Stopping Simulation}
\begin{lstlisting}
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import beta

def bayesian_sequential_test(p_a_true, p_b_true,
                            max_n=20000,
                            check_interval=500,
                            prob_threshold=0.95,
                            min_effect=0.002):
    """
    Simulate Bayesian sequential A/B test with early stopping.

    Parameters:
    -----------
    p_a_true, p_b_true : float
        True conversion rates
    max_n : int
        Maximum sample size per variant
    check_interval : int
        How often to check stopping criteria
    prob_threshold : float
        Probability threshold for stopping
    min_effect : float
        Minimum detectable effect

    Returns:
    --------
    dict : Stopping results
    """
    # Initialize
    x_a, x_b = 0, 0
    n_a, n_b = 0, 0
    stopped = False
    reason = None

    # Prior parameters
    prior_alpha, prior_beta = 1, 1

    # Simulate data collection
    for n in range(check_interval, max_n + 1, check_interval):
        # Generate new data
        new_x_a = np.random.binomial(check_interval, p_a_true)
        new_x_b = np.random.binomial(check_interval, p_b_true)

        x_a += new_x_a
        x_b += new_x_b
        n_a += check_interval
        n_b += check_interval

        # Posterior parameters
        post_alpha_a = prior_alpha + x_a
        post_beta_a = prior_beta + n_a - x_a
        post_alpha_b = prior_alpha + x_b
        post_beta_b = prior_beta + n_b - x_b

        # Sample from posteriors
        samples_a = np.random.beta(post_alpha_a, post_beta_a, 10000)
        samples_b = np.random.beta(post_alpha_b, post_beta_b, 10000)

        # Compute decision metrics
        prob_b_better = np.mean(samples_b > samples_a)
        expected_diff = np.mean(samples_b - samples_a)

        # Check stopping criteria
        if prob_b_better > prob_threshold:
            stopped = True
            reason = f'High confidence B better (P = {prob_b_better:.3f})'
            break
        elif prob_b_better < (1 - prob_threshold):
            stopped = True
            reason = f'High confidence A better (P = {1-prob_b_better:.3f})'
            break
        elif n > 5000 and abs(expected_diff) < min_effect:
            stopped = True
            reason = f'Effect too small (E[diff] = {expected_diff:.5f})'
            break

    if not stopped:
        reason = f'Reached maximum sample size (n = {max_n})'

    return {
        'stopped': stopped,
        'reason': reason,
        'n_total': n_a + n_b,
        'n_per_variant': n_a,
        'conversions_a': x_a,
        'conversions_b': x_b,
        'observed_rate_a': x_a / n_a,
        'observed_rate_b': x_b / n_b,
        'prob_b_better': prob_b_better
    }

# Simulation: Compare to fixed sample size
np.random.seed(42)

# Scenario 1: Large effect (easy to detect early)

print(f"Stopped: {result1['reason']}")
print(f"Total sample size: {result1['n_total']}")
print(f"P(B > A): {result1['prob_b_better']:.4f}\n")

# Scenario 2: Small effect (needs more data)

result2 = bayesian_sequential_test(p_a_true=0.07, p_b_true=0.072)
print(f"Stopped: {result2['reason']}")
print(f"Total sample size: {result2['n_total']}")
print(f"P(B > A): {result2['prob_b_better']:.4f}\n")

# Scenario 3: No effect
print("Scenario 3: No effect (7% vs. 7%)")

print(f"Stopped: {result3['reason']}")
print(f"Total sample size: {result3['n_total']}")

\subsection{Risk-Based Decision Making}

Expected loss provides a framework for risk-based decisions that accounts for both statistical evidence and business consequences.

\begin{example}[Cost-Benefit Analysis with Expected Loss]
A company tests a new checkout flow:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Expected lift: 1.2 percentage points
    \item Expected loss if wrong: 0.15 percentage points
    \item Annual revenue impact of 1 percentage point: \$500,000
    \item Implementation cost: \$100,000
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Expected value analysis:}
\begin{align}
\text{Expected benefit} &= 0.012 \times \$500{,}000 = \$6{,}000 \\
\text{Expected regret} &= 0.0015 \times \$500{,}000 = \$750 \\
\text{Net expected value} &= \$6{,}000 - \$750 - \$100{,}000 = -\$94{,}750
\end{align}

Despite statistical significance, the expected value is negative! The implementation cost exceeds expected benefit after accounting for uncertainty.

This framework integrates statistical and business considerations coherently.
\end{example}

\textbf{Value of Information:}

The expected value of perfect information (EVPI) quantifies how much we'd pay to eliminate uncertainty:
\begin{equation}
\text{EVPI} = \text{Expected loss with current information}
\end{equation}

If EVPI < cost of additional data collection, stop testing and decide now. Otherwise, continue collecting data.

The entire posterior distribution is available for downstream analyses:

    \item Visualize full uncertainty, not just point estimates
    \item Combine with other probabilistic models (e.g., user lifetime value)
\end{itemize}

\begin{lstlisting}
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
from scipy.stats import beta
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def frequentist_test(x_a, n_a, x_b, n_b, alpha=0.05):
    """Two-proportion z-test."""
    p_a = x_a / n_a
    p_b = x_b / n_b
    p_pool = (x_a + x_b) / (n_a + n_b)

    se = np.sqrt(p_pool * (1 - p_pool) * (1/n_a + 1/n_b))
    z = (p_b - p_a) / se
    p_value = 2 * (1 - stats.norm.cdf(abs(z)))

    reject_null = p_value < alpha
    ci = (p_b - p_a) + np.array([-1, 1]) * stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha/2) * se

    return {
        'reject_null': reject_null,
        'p_value': p_value,
        'ci': ci,
        'effect': p_b - p_a
    }

def bayesian_test(x_a, n_a, x_b, n_b, prior_alpha=1, prior_beta=1):
    """Bayesian test with Beta-Binomial model."""
    post_alpha_a = prior_alpha + x_a
    post_beta_a = prior_beta + n_a - x_a
    post_alpha_b = prior_alpha + x_b
    post_beta_b = prior_beta + n_b - x_b

    # Monte Carlo
    samples_a = np.random.beta(post_alpha_a, post_beta_a, 100000)
    samples_b = np.random.beta(post_alpha_b, post_beta_b, 100000)

    prob_b_better = np.mean(samples_b > samples_a)
    diff = samples_b - samples_a
    ci = np.percentile(diff, [2.5, 97.5])

    # Decision: "significant" if P(B > A) > 0.95
    decision = prob_b_better > 0.95 or prob_b_better < 0.05

    return {
        'decision': decision,
        'prob_b_better': prob_b_better,
        'ci': ci,
        'effect': np.mean(diff)
    }

# Simulation study
np.random.seed(42)
n_sims = 1000
sample_sizes = [500, 1000, 2000, 5000]
true_effects = [0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03]  # True differences

results = []

for n_per_variant in sample_sizes:
    for true_effect in true_effects:
        p_a_true = 0.05
        p_b_true = p_a_true + true_effect

        freq_rejections = 0
        bayes_decisions = 0

        for _ in range(n_sims):
            # Generate data
            x_a = np.random.binomial(n_per_variant, p_a_true)
            x_b = np.random.binomial(n_per_variant, p_b_true)

            # Frequentist test
            freq_result = frequentist_test(x_a, n_per_variant,
                                          x_b, n_per_variant)
            if freq_result['reject_null']:
                freq_rejections += 1

            # Bayesian test
            bayes_result = bayesian_test(x_a, n_per_variant,
                                        x_b, n_per_variant)
            if bayes_result['decision']:
                bayes_decisions += 1

        freq_rate = freq_rejections / n_sims
        bayes_rate = bayes_decisions / n_sims

        results.append({
            'n': n_per_variant,
            'true_effect': true_effect,
            'frequentist_rate': freq_rate,
            'bayesian_rate': bayes_rate
        })

        print(f"n={n_per_variant}, effect={true_effect:.3f}: "
              f"Freq={freq_rate:.3f}, Bayes={bayes_rate:.3f}")

# Visualize
import pandas as pd

df = pd.DataFrame(results)

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(14, 10))
axes = axes.flatten()

for i, n in enumerate(sample_sizes):
    ax = axes[i]
    df_n = df[df['n'] == n]

    ax.plot(df_n['true_effect'], df_n['frequentist_rate'],
           'o-', label='Frequentist', linewidth=2)
    ax.plot(df_n['true_effect'], df_n['bayesian_rate'],
           's--', label='Bayesian', linewidth=2)
    ax.axhline(0.05, color='red', linestyle=':',
              label='Nominal Type I error rate')
    ax.set_xlabel('True Effect Size')
    ax.set_ylabel('Rejection/Decision Rate')
    ax.set_title(f'Sample Size = {n} per variant')
    ax.legend()
    ax.grid(alpha=0.3)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('frequentist_vs_bayesian_comparison.pdf')
plt.show()

print("\nInterpretation:")
print("- When true effect = 0: Rejection rate approximates Type I error")
print("- When true effect > 0: Rejection rate approximates Power")
print("- Bayesian tends to be more conservative with uniform priors")
print("- Both approaches converge to high power with large effects/samples")

\subsection{Computational Considerations}

\textbf{Frequentist Computation:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Closed-form solutions for common tests (t-test, z-test, chi-square)
    \item Very fast: milliseconds for standard tests
    \item Scales easily to massive datasets
    \item No convergence diagnostics needed
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Bayesian Computation:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Conjugate models: analytical, as fast as frequentist
    \item Monte Carlo: moderate cost, seconds to minutes
    \item MCMC: can be slow (minutes to hours for complex models)
    \item Requires convergence diagnostics (R-hat, ESS)
    \item Parallelizable across chains
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Practical Guidelines:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Simple A/B test with Beta-Binomial: Bayesian analytical (milliseconds)
    \item Multivariate/hierarchical: Bayesian MCMC (minutes) or frequentist approximations
    \item Real-time dashboards: Frequentist or Bayesian analytical only
    \item Offline analysis: Either approach feasible
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary}

Bayesian A/B testing offers a powerful alternative framework to frequentist methods:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Direct probability statements:} "85\% probability B is better" vs. indirect p-values
    \item \textbf{Prior incorporation:} Leverage historical data and expert knowledge
    \item \textbf{Sequential testing:} Check anytime without inflating error rates
    \item \textbf{Risk quantification:} Expected loss guides decisions
    \item \textbf{Full uncertainty:} Entire posterior distribution available
    \item \textbf{Flexible:} Analytical conjugate models or MCMC for complex cases
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Trade-offs:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Requires prior specification and sensitivity analysis
    \item Can be computationally intensive (MCMC)
    \item Less familiar to many stakeholders
    \item No Type I/II error rate guarantees (different paradigm)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Recommendation:} Learn both frameworks. Choose based on organizational context, stakeholder preferences, regulatory requirements, and testing needs. Hybrid approaches combining frequentist confirmatory tests with Bayesian decision analysis are increasingly common and capture benefits of both paradigms.

    \begin{enumerate}

        \end{itemize}
